The honest answer is "it depends" — here's what it depends on

Every agency's pricing page says this, and it's true, which makes it useless on its own. What actually moves the number is a short list of factors — and once you know what they are, you can get a realistic range for your own project in about five minutes, without talking to a single salesperson.

The four things that actually drive cost

1. Scope — how many distinct things it needs to do

A tool that does one thing well (log an entry, generate a report, route a request) costs a fraction of a platform with a dozen interconnected features. The biggest cost driver isn't "how complex is one feature" — it's how many features there are and how much they depend on each other.

2. Integrations

Connecting to a payment processor, a CRM, an existing database, or a third-party API each adds real time — not because the code is hard, but because someone else's system rarely behaves exactly like its documentation says it will. A project with three integrations reliably costs more than an identical project with zero.

3. Design complexity

A clean internal tool with a handful of screens is quick to design. A consumer-facing product with a distinct visual identity, animations, and a design system built to scale is a separate, real cost — often underestimated because it's easy to confuse "looks simple" with "was simple to design."

4. Team composition and timeline

A solo developer working over four months and a five-person team shipping in three weeks can build the same thing — at very different price points, and with different risk profiles. Compressed timelines cost more per hour of work delivered, not less.

Fixed-price vs. time-and-materials

A fixed price sounds safer, but it only works when the scope is genuinely fixed — which is rare before any real design work has happened. Agencies that quote a hard fixed price on a vague scope are usually padding the number to cover their own risk, and you're paying for that padding whether the risk materializes or not. Time-and-materials with a not-to-exceed ceiling and weekly visibility into hours is usually the more honest structure once a project has real scope behind it.

Red flags in a quote

  • A number given before any discovery conversation about what you're actually trying to solve.
  • No breakdown by phase or feature — just one lump sum.
  • A price that's dramatically lower than every other quote you've received, with no explanation of what's different about their approach.
  • No mention of who owns the code and the intellectual property once the project ends.

The fastest way to get an actual number for your project — not a range — is a short conversation about what you're building.
