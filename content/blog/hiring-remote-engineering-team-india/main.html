The pitch is always the same. The real question is what to check

Lower cost, a large talent pool, English-speaking teams — the pitch for hiring a remote engineering team in India is consistent across almost every vendor, and it's largely true. It's also not the part that determines whether the engagement actually works. The part that matters is what you check before committing, not whether the general pitch is accurate.

What to actually check

Time zone overlap, specifically

Not "do they work remotely" but how many real hours of overlap exist with your working day, and whether that's enough for the kind of collaboration your project needs — daily standups, quick clarifying questions, or mostly async handoffs. India is roughly 9.5–13.5 hours ahead of the US and 3.5–5.5 hours ahead of Western Europe depending on the country and the season; a team that schedules calls inside your hours rather than asking you to join theirs at midnight is doing real work to make that gap workable.

Who you're actually getting

Find out whether the person pitching you is the person who'll build it, or whether it gets handed to a team you haven't met once signed. Ask directly whether the team is dedicated to your project or split across several accounts at once.

Data security and where your information actually sits

Ask what access controls exist, whether an NDA is standard practice before sensitive scoping conversations, and where code and data are hosted during development. A team that has a clear, specific answer to this is different from one that says "we take security seriously" and stops there.

Code and IP ownership, in writing

You should own the code and intellectual property for anything custom-built, confirmed in the contract before work starts — not implied, not assumed, written down.

Communication cadence, not just "good communication"

What's the actual rhythm — a weekly call, a shared project channel, async written updates? Vague promises of "good communication" mean nothing until they're a specific, scheduled pattern you can hold the team to.

Red flags, specific to this kind of engagement

  • A quote given before any real conversation about what you're building.
  • No named point of contact — every question goes to a generic inbox.
  • Reluctance to put code ownership or data handling in writing before a contract.
  • No client references, and no explanation for why not.
