Both are legitimate — for different jobs

Low-code and no-code platforms get dismissed by developers and over-sold by vendors, and neither reaction is useful. They're good tools for a specific class of problem and a poor fit for another. The useful question isn't which is better in general — it's which one matches what you're actually trying to build.

When low-code or no-code is the smart choice

Internal tools with a small, known set of users

Approval workflows, simple dashboards, request trackers and admin panels used by a handful of employees are exactly where these platforms shine. The requirements are clear, the audience is forgiving, and speed matters more than polish.

You're validating an idea before committing to a build

If the real question is "will anyone use this?", a working prototype in a few weeks answers it far more cheaply than a full custom build. Treat it as a learning tool, not the final product.

The process is standard and the platform already models it

When your workflow looks like what the platform was designed for — forms, approvals, basic CRM, scheduling — you get most of the value without building anything from scratch.

When custom software earns its cost

The system is part of what makes your business different

If your operating model, pricing logic or customer experience is a competitive advantage, forcing it into a platform's built-in assumptions tends to erode exactly what makes it valuable.

You expect real scale or complex integrations

Low-code platforms have ceilings on performance, data volume and how flexibly they connect to other systems. Hitting those ceilings mid-growth is expensive because migration is a rebuild.

You need full control over data, security and hosting

Regulated industries, sensitive data and strict compliance requirements are often easier to satisfy when you own the whole stack rather than depending on a vendor's roadmap and policies.

Per-seat pricing will outgrow the value

Platform licensing is usually charged per user or per app. What looks cheap for ten users can cost more than owning the software once you're at several hundred.

The hybrid most teams settle on

In practice the choice is rarely all-or-nothing. A common pattern is to run internal, low-stakes workflows on a low-code platform while building the customer-facing product or core operational system as custom software, connected through APIs. You get speed where speed matters and control where control matters, and you avoid paying custom-build prices for a simple approval form.
