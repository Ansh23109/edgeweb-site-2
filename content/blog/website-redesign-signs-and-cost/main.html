"It still works" is a low bar for a website

A website can load fine, have no broken links, and still be actively costing a business customers — because looking dated, loading slowly on mobile, or failing to explain what you do in five seconds does real damage that never shows up in an uptime report. The question isn't whether the site works. It's whether it's still doing its job.

Six signs it's time for a redesign, not a patch

1. It wasn't built mobile-first, and it shows

If a site was designed for desktop and mobile was an afterthought, no amount of small fixes solves that — the layout logic itself needs to change, and most traffic today arrives on a phone.

2. Your business has changed and the site hasn't

New services, a repositioned brand, or a different ideal customer than when the site was built all mean the site is now describing a business you don't quite run anymore.

3. Updating it requires calling a developer for a text change

If nobody on your team can update a price, a page, or a piece of copy without outside help, the site is costing you agility every time something needs to change.

4. It's slow, and you already know it

If you've ever waited for your own homepage to load and felt impatient, your customers feel that too — and unlike you, they don't have a reason to wait it out.

5. Analytics show high bounce, low time-on-page, or a broken funnel

Traffic arriving and immediately leaving, or dropping off at a specific step, is usually a design and clarity problem before it's a marketing problem — more traffic to a leaky funnel doesn't fix the leak.

6. You're embarrassed to send someone the link

This one is subjective, but it's rarely wrong. If your instinct is to explain away the website before sharing it, your customers are noticing the same thing without the explanation.

Redesign vs. refresh: not always the same project

Not every one of these signs needs a full rebuild. A refresh — updated visuals, better copy, faster images, on the existing structure — solves problems 2, 4 and 6 quickly and cheaply. A full redesign — new information architecture, new tech stack, rebuilt from the ground up — is what's actually needed for problems 1 and 3, where the underlying structure itself is the issue, not just how it looks.

What to have ready before you talk to anyone about a redesign

  • What the site needs to actually do for the business — leads, sales, bookings, credibility — not just "look better."
  • Analytics access, even basic Google Analytics, so a real conversation about what's underperforming is possible.
  • Any brand material that already exists — logo files, past design work, even a Pinterest board of sites you like.
  • A realistic sense of what content (copy, images, product data) already exists versus what needs to be created from scratch, since that's a bigger cost driver than most people expect.
