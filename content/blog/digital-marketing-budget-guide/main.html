"10% of revenue" is a rule of thumb, not an answer

It's the number every article quotes, and it's not wrong exactly — it's just not useful on its own. Two businesses at the same revenue, in different stages of growth and different competitive positions, should not spend the same amount on marketing. The useful version of this question is what actually drives the number up or down for your specific situation.

What actually drives the number

Business stage

A new business trying to establish any market presence typically needs to spend a higher percentage of revenue on marketing than an established one with existing word-of-mouth and repeat customers — often in the range of 12–20% for an early-stage business versus 6–10% for an established one, though these ranges vary significantly by industry.

How competitive the category is

Ranking or advertising in a crowded category — generic e-commerce, local services in a dense city — costs more per customer than a specialized niche with less competition for the same attention.

Customer lifetime value

A business where a customer is worth a one-time $50 purchase can't justify the same acquisition spend as one where a customer is worth $5,000 over several years. Budget should scale with what a customer is actually worth, not with a flat percentage alone.

Sales cycle length

A long B2B sales cycle means marketing spend today shows results months from now — budgeting has to account for that lag instead of judging a campaign's success after four weeks.

A rough framework, not a formula

StageTypical range (% of revenue)Where it tends to go
Early-stage / pre-product-market-fit12–20%Testing channels, building initial SEO and content, paid experiments
Growing, repeatable sales8–15%Scaling the channels already proven to work
Established, steady growth6–10%Maintaining visibility, retention, brand

These are ranges commonly cited across the industry, not a quote — the right number for a specific business depends on the factors above more than the stage alone.

Where the budget actually splits

  • SEO and content — slower to show results, compounds over time, lower ongoing cost per visitor once it's working.
  • Paid search and social — faster results, costs scale directly with spend, stops the moment you stop paying.
  • Conversion rate optimization — often the highest-leverage spend nobody budgets for separately, because it makes every other channel's traffic worth more without buying more of it.
  • Brand and creative — compounds slowly, hardest to attribute directly, genuinely matters for anything beyond pure direct-response.

The real mistake, either direction

Underspending shows up as invisible — not enough volume in any channel to tell what's actually working, so every decision is a guess. Overspending shows up as diminishing returns in one channel while a cheaper one goes untested. Both mistakes come from picking a number before understanding what it needs to buy, which is the same problem as picking a percentage without checking it against your own numbers first.
